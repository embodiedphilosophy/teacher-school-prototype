Yoga is a living tradition of knowledge. It deserves an institution that teaches it like one.

That sentence is the whole argument. The rest of this page is the small print.

## Why the industry needs this

Every year, a great many people finish a 200-hour training, receive a certificate and are told they are teachers. They are not entirely wrong. They have learned a sequence, a vocabulary and the nerve to say "inhale" in front of strangers. What they have not been given is the thing every other serious discipline assumes: a long apprenticeship under people who can see what they are doing wrong.

Your 200-hour certificate is a learner's permit. It says you may practise on the road in daylight with someone sitting next to you. Somewhere in the last few decades we agreed to treat it as a licence, and then we were surprised by the traffic.

The training was never meant to be the end. It was meant to be the beginning of a path with markers on it: a second stage where you are taught the sources, a third where you specialise, and a fourth where you are trusted to teach the teachers. Medicine has this. Law has this. Carpentry has this. Yoga, which has been handing knowledge from one person to another for longer than any of them, mostly has weekend workshops.

The result is a profession of devoted, under-supported people who are asked to be philosophers, anatomists, counsellors and small-business owners at once, and who are rarely observed doing any of it. They teach hundreds of classes before anyone tells them what they are doing well. Many leave. Many stay and quietly stop learning. Some of the best become excellent at hiding what they don't know, which is its own kind of tragedy.

Nobody is to blame for this. It is simply what happens when a tradition grows faster than the institutions around it. But the tradition is not the problem. The tradition is where the answers are. What is missing is a school.

> Your 200-hour certificate is a learner's permit.

## What we refuse to do

We refuse to promise you anything about your chakras. We have no position on crystals. We are fond of incense on its own merits.

We refuse to flatter you. A feedback session at this school will tell you what worked, what didn't and what to read before next week. Nobody has ever improved from being told they were wonderful. If you want to be told you are wonderful, there is a very good café on the corner.

We refuse to teach the tradition as a mood. The Yoga Sūtra is not a poster. It is a compressed, argumentative, difficult text with a commentarial history spanning fifteen centuries, and it deserves to be read the way you would read Kant: slowly, with arguments, and with a dictionary open. The same goes for the Tantras, the Upaniṣads and the long, quarrelsome literature that grew up around them. We will not pretend they agree with each other. We will not pretend they agree with us.

We refuse to treat bodies as problems. Anatomy at this school is a set of working hypotheses, not a list of things you have been doing wrong since childhood. "Tight hips" is not a diagnosis. "Misaligned" is not a moral failing. We will teach you what is known, say plainly where the evidence runs out and trust you to be an adult about it.

We refuse to let you teach unobserved. This is the rule that will cost us the most students and the one we are least willing to bend.

We refuse the word "journey," for the same reason we refuse "elevate" and "bliss." Not because the experiences are fake, but because the vocabulary has been worn smooth by people selling retreats.

> No crystals. Some Sanskrit. A lot of feedback.

## What we commit to

We commit to a curriculum with a spine. There are seven departments, because yoga is not one subject: philosophy and texts, āsana and method, movement science, breath and meditation, teaching and pedagogy, therapeutics, and Sanskrit and chant. Every student studies across all of them before choosing depth in one. Nobody specialises early. Nobody graduates knowing only the thing they already liked.

We commit to mentorship as the structure of the school and not a perk at the margin of it. Every student belongs to a pod of four to six teachers and a master, from the first course to the capstone. You will be seen. You will be written about, carefully and honestly. You will write back. Over the years the record of that exchange becomes the most truthful account of your teaching that you will ever have, and it is yours to keep.

We commit to teaching from the sources. That means translations with their assumptions on the table, Sanskrit for those who want it and a standing invitation to disagree with the person at the front of the room, provided you have read the footnote.

We commit to honesty about what yoga can do. It can steady an anxious person for an hour. It can make a stiff person more comfortable in their body. It can give a lifetime of attention a shape. It does not cure cancer, and any teacher who implies otherwise is practising medicine without a licence and without our blessing. Our therapeutics department teaches scope first and technique second, because the most important thing a teacher can learn is when to say, "This is beyond what I should do, and here is who to see."

We commit to pay and treat our faculty like colleagues, to publish how the school is governed and to give our students a real say in how it changes. A school that asks for rigour from its students and none from itself is a brand.

We commit to being small enough to know your name. If we ever aren't, we will say so and stop admitting people until we are.

> Mentorship isn't a perk. It's the whole point.

## What a teacher is

A teacher is not a guru. A teacher is not a performer, a therapist or a brand. A teacher is someone who has taken responsibility for another person's learning and is willing to be accountable for how it goes.

That is a smaller claim than it sounds and a harder one to meet. It means you know your subject well enough to say what you don't know. It means you can watch a room and see who is lost. It means you can explain, in plain language, why you asked a student to do what you asked, and change your mind when the answer is no longer good enough. It means you are still a student of someone.

A teacher is formed in the room, under someone's eye. That is the oldest pedagogy we have, and the one the industry dropped. We would like to pick it back up. We would like to do so in a way that is serious without being solemn, learned without being precious and funny where the material permits, which is more often than the brochures suggest.

We do not claim to have invented any of this. Every idea in this document was practised by someone, somewhere, before we wrote it down. What we are offering is an institution: a place with a door, a timetable, a library, a body of faculty who have agreed to be answerable to one another, and a duty of care to the people who walk in.

Come with a notebook. Bring your worst class. We will start there.

> A teacher is formed in the room, under someone's eye.
